# Chapter 2: Choosing a printer sensibly

The best printer is boring: it's always ready, its supplies cost little, and you never think about it. Here's how to buy boring on purpose.

## The first question: what do you actually print?

| Your reality | Your machine | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mostly documents, receipts, forms; occasional color | **Monochrome laser (all-in-one optional)** | Bulletproof, cheap per page, never dries out |
| Photos, flyers, school projects (photos matter) | **Inkjet with separate ink tanks** | Best color, but supplies need regular use |
| Print 10–50 pages/week, mixed color and black | **Ink tank (refillable) all-in-one** | Color quality plus dirt-cheap per-page ink |
| Seldom print—once a month or less | **Print shop / office + a scan app** | The cheapest printer is one you don't own |
| Small business and you print constantly | **Color laser or high-volume tank printer** | Per-page cost dominates everything else |

If that table ends your shopping: good. Everything below is the reasoning.

## Inkjet vs. laser, in plain terms

**Inkjet** sprays liquid ink through tiny nozzles.

- Pros: superb photo color, cheap to buy, small, good on plain and photo paper.
- Cons: ink dries if neglected; page cost is high unless you use refillable tanks; speed is modest.

**Laser** fuses dry toner powder with heat.

- Pros: fast, crisp text, toner can't dry out while you ignore it, low cost per page for black-and-white.
- Cons: heavier, more electricity when printing, color lasers are pricier, full-page photo gloss is not their thing.

**Rule of thumb:** if you print color rarely but documents always, buy the mono laser. If you print photos regularly, buy an ink tank inkjet. Everything else is a compromise between those two poles.

## The cost-per-page math (a preview of chapter 5)

A $49 inkjet with $40 cartridges is a $49 down payment on supplies that quickly dwarf the box. A laser with a $55 toner cartridge that lasts 3,000 pages costs about 2¢ per page. Do not buy a printer without knowing two numbers from the spec sheet:

1. **Cartridge yield** (pages per cartridge), and
2. **Cartridge price.**

Divide them. That's your real price of admission, forever. Chapter 5 walks through full examples.

## Features that actually matter

Worth paying for:

- **Wi-Fi with Wi-Fi Direct** (so a phone can print even without a network).
- **Automatic duplex** (double-sided printing). Saves paper, makes booklets possible.
- **All-in-one capability** with an **automatic document feeder (ADF)** if you ever scan multi-page stacks.
- **Separate ink cartridges** (or tanks) so one empty color doesn't kill the rest.
- **Drivers that exist for your operating system**—check the maker's site before buying, not after.

Worth ignoring:

- "Free ink month" bundles that quietly become an ink subscription after the trial (read the terms, and don't enroll by accident).
- ADF duplex scanning unless you scan double-sided stacks weekly.
- Bluetooth printing (Wi-Fi is more reliable and universal).
- QAM numbers, dots-per-inch wars, and other spec-sheet stardust. All modern printers make text anyone can read.

## The shopping sentence

You don't need a model name from this handbook—that ages badly. You need a *sentence* to say to a search engine or a store clerk:

> "A monochrome laser all-in-one with duplex, Wi-Fi, and cheap high-yield toner under $250."
>
> "A refillable ink tank all-in-one with duplex and an ADF under $400."

Both sentences return good machines from every major brand.

## Buying used (a quick word)

Lasers buy well used; inkjets don't. Toner is sealed powder that survives shelf time; ink heads clog in storage. A $60 five-year-old laser with a fresh toner cartridge is often the quietly best deal in printing.

## Before you pay

- Confirm driver/support pages exist for the exact model (a 10-year support window is a good sign).
- Confirm Wi-Fi *and* USB ports—you'll want the cable during setup hassles, as chapter 3 explains.
- Price the **high-yield** cartridge, not the starter cartridge that comes in the box. Starter cartridges are the "free sample" of printing.
